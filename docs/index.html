The idea

TL;DR FB learns the successor representation (SR) through a low-rank bottleneck, but continuous dynamics are high-rank. Repeating actions acts as a low-pass filter: slow, room-scale modes survive, fast ones die out, and the SR becomes low-rank enough for FB to learn it stably.

Overview

Three ways to a low-rank SR

Three ways to make the SR low-rank: force a low rank, raise $\gamma$, or repeat actions. With exact dynamics all three work. When FB has to learn the SR in continuous space, only temporal abstraction gives a correct, long-horizon Q-function.

Look for: the outlined row, the learned Q with the goal at ★.
  • Low-rank: a small bump at ★, then flat and patchy. No value reaches the other rooms.
  • High $\gamma$: dark almost everywhere, with false peaks in the far rooms. Nothing points back to ★.
  • Temporal abstraction: one smooth slope from ★ out through the doorways.

Abstract

Forward–backward (FB) representations provide a powerful framework for learning the successor representation (SR) in continuous spaces by enforcing a low-rank factorization. However, a fundamental spectral mismatch often exists between the high-rank transition dynamics of continuous environments and the low-rank bottleneck of the FB architecture, making accurate low-rank representation learning difficult. In this work, we analyze temporal abstraction as a mechanism to mitigate this mismatch. By characterizing the spectral properties of the transition operator, we show that temporal abstraction acts analogously to a low-pass filter that suppresses high-frequency spectral components. This suppression reduces the effective rank of the induced SR while preserving a formal bound on the resulting value function error. Empirically, we show that this alignment is a key factor for stable FB learning, particularly at high discount factors where bootstrapping becomes error-prone. Our results identify temporal abstraction as a principled mechanism for shaping the spectral structure of the underlying MDP and enabling effective long-horizon representations in continuous control.

Why does repeating actions help? ↓

Mechanism

Temporal abstraction as a low-pass filter

Repeating an action $k$ times turns $P$ into $P^k$: same eigenvectors, eigenvalues $\lambda \to \lambda^k$. Slow, room-scale modes ($\lambda \approx 1$) survive; fast ones die out. Shown for the four-rooms grid world.

Look for: slide $k$. The grey dots ($k = 1$) spread over many modes; the blue dots collapse onto $\mu = 1$ except a few. Everything right of the FB rank line is what FB can't hold; the readout below shows how much of the SR that is. Hover a dot to see its mode on the grid.
